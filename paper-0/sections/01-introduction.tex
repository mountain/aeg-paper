The earliest presentations of Arithmetic Expression Geometry began with a
simple picture: a nested arithmetic expression can be read as a sequence of
updates of one current value, and that sequence can be drawn as a path.  This
picture was developed for one comb orientation.  The opposite comb orientation
was visible in the syntax but was not given an equally explicit geometry.
Division shows that the omission is substantive.  Dividing the evolving value
by a constant remains affine, whereas dividing a constant by the evolving
value introduces inversion, a pole, and the projective point at infinity.

This paper supplies the elementary prelude that is missing between ordinary
arithmetic syntax and the formal marked-history theory of Paper~I.  Its main
objects are two recursively defined combs:
\[
  L_k[z]=\omega_k(L_{k-1}[z],c_k),
  \qquad
  R_k[z]=\omega_k(c_k,R_{k-1}[z]),
  \qquad L_0[z]=R_0[z]=z.
\]
The index is chronological: the operation with index $1$ is evaluated first,
and the operation with index $k$ is the $k$th layer added around the already
computed subtree.  Thus a left-expanded comb carries its active subtree in the
first operand slot, while a right-expanded comb carries it in the second.
This explicit convention is used throughout; no conclusion depends on an
ambiguous planar drawing convention.

The two combs have the same dependency shape: their internal vertices form a
chain, so their internal evaluation order is unique.  They differ in the
one-hole maps attached to that chain.  For a binary operation $\omega$ and a
constant $c$, the two sections are
\[
  C^{(1)}_{\omega,c}[z]=\omega(z,c),
  \qquad
  C^{(2)}_{\omega,c}[z]=\omega(c,z).
\]
For addition and multiplication the two sections agree.  For subtraction they
remain affine but differ by a reflection.  For division they separate sharply:
\[
  C^{(1)}_{/,c}[z]=\frac{z}{c},
  \qquad
  C^{(2)}_{/,c}[z]=\frac{c}{z}.
\]
The first fixes infinity; the second exchanges zero and infinity.

This algebraic difference suggests two complementary geometric readings.  A
left-expanded affine expression is naturally read covariantly: choose an
initial point and push it forward through the successive updates.  On the
basic upper-half-plane model this gives the familiar zigzag \emph{point path}.
A right-expanded projective expression is naturally read contravariantly:
choose an output horocycle and pull it back through the successive outer
contexts.  The inverse images are parallel lines when the current operator has
its pole at infinity and nested circles when the pole is finite.  We call the
resulting one-parameter family a \emph{ripple pencil}.  The term is descriptive;
the underlying objects are ordinary horocycles and their M\"obius pullbacks.

The paper has three aims.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item It gives the left and right combs in parallel, proves their elementary
  correspondence through opposite operations, and identifies exactly where
  division destroys affine symmetry.

  \item It develops two elementary pictures on the same upper-half-plane
  background: a point path in the zeroth-kind arithmetic expression space and
  a ripple pencil tangent at the pole of a fractional linear operator.

  \item It unifies the pictures by $2\times2$ matrices.  Prefix products push
  points forward; the same matrices pull horocycles back, determine poles and
  fixed points, and place the affine stabilizer of infinity inside
  $\PGL_2$.
\end{enumerate}

Reciprocals and iteration provide the bridge between the finite and infinite
pictures.  The scalar identity
\[
  \frac{1}{1-r}=1+r+r^2+\cdots
\]
is treated as a convergence statement, not a formal equality without
hypotheses.  Its finite truncations satisfy a fixed-point recursion, and the
finite fixed point $1/(1-r)$ escapes to infinity at $r=1$.  Replacing the
affine recursion by $z\mapsto a+b/z$ gives finite continued fractions and a
quadratic fixed-point equation.  These examples make poles, infinity, and
iterative closure visible before the more general projective and dynamical
machinery is introduced.

The status boundary is important.  Everything proved here is elementary
algebra, upper-half-plane geometry, or local one-dimensional dynamics.  The
paper does not define a new projective metric, a projective arithmetic
expression space, or a general convergence theory for infinite expressions.
Ordinary division remains partial even when its M\"obius continuation is
well-defined on the projective line.  Paper~I retains the marked-history
formalism, the classification of sequential trees, projective evaluation of
bilateral histories, affine flow, torsion, and contact curvature.  The present
paper prepares those constructions without revising their terminology.

The organization follows the mathematical dependency.  Section~\ref{sec:p0-two-combs}
fixes the two combs and their one-hole algebra.  Section~\ref{sec:p0-paths}
constructs the point-path picture in the basic model.  Section~\ref{sec:p0-ripples}
derives ripple pencils from M\"obius pullback.  Section~\ref{sec:p0-reciprocals}
treats reciprocals, poles, infinity, geometric-series closure, and fixed
points.  Section~\ref{sec:p0-projective-unification} gives the matrix and
projective synthesis, and Section~\ref{sec:p0-interface} records the precise
interface exported to Paper~I.
